4. Judge questions and answers

QuestionAnswer
How do you get to $1M?Funnel on slide 7. 14 annual contracts at ~$60k average plus implementation and support add-ons. Rates come from our GTM plan: 30% target-to-discovery, 50% demo-to-pilot, 60–65% pilot-to-annual. We recheck them monthly; Vanderbilt is the first data point.
Why $60k average if the price is $18/student?Early deals start at the $30k minimum or with one department; full-campus deals at mid-size schools run $100k–$250k. $60k is a blended first-year number.
How do you know the sales plan works?Three signals: a paying first customer, a buyer path we’ve already walked (security review, DPA, measures), and leading indicators we report monthly. If discovery-to-pilot falls under 25% for two months, we change the segment or message.
Why will schools pay when they have EAB Navigate?Navigate is built for staff. We’re student-facing and sit beside it. Nothing is replaced in year one, and the pilot is small enough to approve from a discretionary budget.
Sales cycles in higher ed are long. How do you survive?We sell a one-term paid pilot, not a campus contract, which shortens the first yes. We also keep burn near $565/month until pilots pay.
What does Vanderbilt pay? Is it signed?[State the confirmed fee and status exactly.] If anything is not signed yet, say so plainly.
What about FERPA and AI?We operate as a school official under a DPA. Student records never reach an AI provider: the gateway drops record data and scans prompts, and we test that before launch.
What if a big vendor copies you?Our edge is trust and fit: source labels on every fact, student-controlled sharing, procurement-ready documents, and one term to prove value. A big vendor can copy features; they can’t easily copy a student-first consent model on top of a staff CRM.
Why you?[Personal story: the problem you lived, what you built, the pilot you sold.]
Who is building it?Founder today, with design and engineering complete to [state honestly]. First engineer hired on the third signed pilot.
Why not raise venture money now?We’d rather prove pilot-to-annual conversion first; it sets a better price for the company. The grant and pilot revenue fund the next 90 days.
What is your biggest risk?Founder capacity. Mitigation: hires triggered by revenue, contractors for legal and finance, and runbooks so delivery doesn’t depend on memory.
What will students actually do with it?Check Today when something changes: registration, a hold, a due date. We measure weekly active students and readiness at window open.
Is the company incorporated?[Yes: entity name and state / In progress through Stripe Atlas, filed on date.] Business bank account at [Rho] is [open / pending].
What does the mentor help with?Introductions to registrars and student-success leaders, pricing and procurement advice, and a sanity check on conversion.

6. Incorporate and bank (do this now)

Get advice before filing. Entity type and tax choices have lasting effects; a short call with an attorney or accountant is worth it.
  1. Choose the entity. Stripe Atlas forms a Delaware C corporation or an LLC. A Delaware C corp is the usual choice if you may raise venture money later. If a Semester LLC already exists, ask counsel whether to keep it or convert.
  2. File through Stripe Atlas (about $500): certificate of incorporation, EIN, founder stock purchase, bylaws, registered agent.
  3. File the 83(b) election within 30 days of the founder stock grant if your shares vest. This deadline cannot be fixed later.
  4. Open a business bank account with Rho (or another bank) using the EIN and formation documents.
  5. Register in Tennessee as a foreign corporation if you operate there; ask the accountant about the Tennessee franchise and excise tax.
  6. Move all Semester money into the business account: pilot invoices, grant, expenses. Nothing through personal accounts.
  7. Update the legal name in the MSA, order form, DPA, privacy policy and invoices.
  8. Send the C4E the legal entity name and banking details for the grant check.
